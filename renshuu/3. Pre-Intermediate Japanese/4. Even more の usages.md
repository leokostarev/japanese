> の

Let's talk about a few more ways to use particle の！

> (Noun) の
> (Adjective) の

The first new usage we're going to talk about in this lesson is actually fairly closely related to the way we already know how to use の.
However, this usage of の replaces a noun, rather than connecting one to another.
If you're talking about something and you've already mentioned a noun, the next time you refer to it, you can simply use (noun)の or (adjective)の to replace it later on.
This could be talking about a person's possessions, or about types of objects.

> この時計は私の時計です → この時計は私のです
> 先生の本を借りる → 先生のを借りる

This could be something as simple as some of the basic sentences we learned early on.
Instead of saying, “This watch is my watch” and being redundant, you could say, “This watch is mine” where the わたしの translates to “mine” instead of “My (thing).”
So, if you're talking about a book, you could say either “I borrow the teacher's book,” or simply, “I borrow the teacher's.”
Just like in English, where you omit the word “book” since you know what you're talking about, in the Japanese, you can just use particle の without the word ほん again.

> きれいな靴を買いました。 → きれいなのを買いました。
> 早い電車に乗った。 → 早いのに乗った。

Similarly, if you're talking about shoes, you could either say, “I bought pretty shoes,” or “I bought pretty ones.”
In this case, you can see the English word “ones” replaces the word 'shoes': similarly, の will replace the word くつ in Japanese.
Notice that, just like the noun was still there, you will still add the な at the end of a な adjective.
If you caught an early train, depending on the context you could either say, “I rode an early train,” or “I rode an early one”.
Notice that in most of these sentences, you have two particles in a row: のを、のに、etc. You also have のです.
This might be confusing at first, as we haven't seen a lot of particles used in combination yet, but just think of the の as replacing a noun, and you'll be fine!

> (Verb)の
> (Verb)こと

But what if you see a verb followed by の, rather than a noun or an adjective?
This will take a verb, and turn it gramatically into a noun!
You will also see a verb with こと at the end, it will work the same.

> 見るの・見ること
> 読むの・読むこと
> するの・すること
> 来るの・来ること

Both of these versions will be used with the dictionary forms of verbs, and essentially will mean “the act of (verb)ing” - seeing, reading, doing, coming, etc.

> 映画を見ることが好きだ。
> その本を読むのは難しかった。

You will often see this kind of sentence ending in です or an adjective, as it is commonly used to describe something about the action.
So if you like watching movies, you would use the first sentence.
You can see that “watching movies,” えいがをみること, functions as a noun here.
Similarly, the second sentence says, “Reading that book was difficult.”

> 運転することができますか？
> ここに来るのは初めてです。

The third sentence asks, “Can you drive?”
And the fourth sentence combines this new concept with the はじめてです that we learned before, to say “It's my first time coming here.”
In most of these sentences, you can see that we use the gerund in English: “watching,” “reading,” “coming”.
The exception is the first sentence above, “Can you drive?” but we'll talk about that specific instance in a later lesson!

> (Casual sentence)の

The last way we'll talk about particle の is using it to mark a question.
This is exactly the same as how we use particle か, except that you can only use の in this way using casual form.

> どうするの？
> 宿題終わったの？

For example, you might hear someone ask, “What will you do?” using the first sentence.
The second sentence says, “Did you finish your homework?”
Notice that in some casual sentences, you can omit particles like は and を.

> 外、寒いの？
> 今暇なの？
> これはカレーなの？

You can also omit words like です and end your phrase with a noun or an adjective (followed by question particle の) as you can see here.
So the first question. (again, omitting the particle は) asks, “Is it cold outside?”
The second question asks, “Are you free now?”
And the last question asks, “Is this curry?”
Using の in casual questions like these sounds much more natural than か, which is more commonly used for polite questions.
You can also omit the particle altogether when asking a casual question, and just use a rising intonation to show you're asking rather than saying!

Hopefully, now you can see particle の coming after nouns, adjectives, or verbs and followed directly by another particle, and you will understand what's happening!
