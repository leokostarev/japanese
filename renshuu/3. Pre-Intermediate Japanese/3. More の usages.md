
> の

Way back, when you first started learning Japanese, you learned that particle の showed possession, or added description to a noun.
But did you know that's not の's only usage?
In the next few lessons, we're going to talk about several more ways that you can use this versatile particle.

> (Noun) の (Noun)  
> (Noun) から/へ/で/と/まで/Etc. の(Noun)

The first new way to use particle の also connects nouns, just like the possessive/descriptive の that you've learned already.
In this case, it's used to modify the second noun with the first.
You can see here that you can also use this pattern in combination with several other particles, including から、へ、で、と、and まで

> 日本語の先生  
> 大学の時

The first part of this, without using any other particles, is essentially the same as what we've learned before: it adds description to a noun.
You've seen the phrase にほんごのせんせい before, you can see that にほんご modifies せんせい, in that it gives you context: what kind of teacher? A Japanese teacher.
In the same way, a phrase like だいがくのとき, which would be translated to English as “When I was in college” or more directly, “(during the) time of college,” “college” is giving you context for what “time” you're talking about - college time!

> 先生からの質問
> 友達へのカード
> 学校での運動会
> 日本までの旅行
> 社長との会話

When you add particles such as the ones seen here, you are still using the first noun to modify the second, but just like in English, you will add another preposition to your modification.
So for example, you might have “a question from the teacher.” The question is the main idea, but what kind of question is it? It's one from the teacher.
You could also have “a card for a friend.” “For a friend” is modifying the noun “card.”
You might also have “an event at school.” What kind of event is it? It's an event (that happens) at school.
Similarly, you could have “a trip to Japan.” What kind of trip is it? It's a trip where the destination is Japan.
Or, you could have “a conversation with (your) boss.” Here, “conversation” is modified by “with (the) boss.”
These modified nouns can be used just like the noun by itself in a sentence!

> 弁護士の山田さん
> 大学生の息子
> 友達のカオちゃん
> カナダ人のナンシーさん

Another way you can use の is to talk about people, usually referencing their jobs or another identifying quality about them.
For example, you might talk about Ms. Yamada, who is a lawyer.
In Japanese, you would simply say べんごしのやまださん or “Yamada, the lawyer.”
You can also use it in a more general way, like だいがくせいのむすこ, "my son who is a college student.”
Or ともだちのカオちゃん, “My friend Kao.”
Or カナダじんのナンシーさん, “Nancy the Canadian.”
You might notice that while in English, the main noun and the qualifying noun can be in either location, in Japanese the main noun always comes after the の, just like we learned before.

> (Noun) が/の (Adjective + Noun)  
> (Noun) が/の (Casual verb + Noun)

You don't just use の when qualifying a noun with another noun: you can also use it to qualify nouns with adjectives or verbs!

> 髪の長い人
> 目のきれいな猫
> 子供が遊ぶゲーム
> 私の読んだ本

To make these qualifiers, that will translate as a relative clause in English, you will have your main noun at the end, as usual.
In front of the main noun, you will either have a a noun, の or が, and an adjective, as in the first two examples; or a noun, の or が, and a verb in the casual form, like you see in the last two examples. Note that の and が here are interchangeable.
So if you're talking about a “person with long hair,” you would use the first phrase.
The second phrase is “a cat with pretty eyes.” Notice the な still will come before the noun here.
The third phrase would be “the game which the children play.”
And the last would be, “the book that I read.”

> 髪の長い人はあそこにいます。
> 私の読んだ本は面白かった。

You can combine a relative clause with a main idea like this.
The first sentence would be “The person who has long hair is over there.” “Who has long hair” is a relative clause that modifies “person.”
The second sentence is “The book that I read was interesting.” “That I read” is the relative clause modifying “book.”

> 髪の長い人は大学の時の友達の鈴木さん。

Here we have “The person with long hair is my friend from college, Mr. Suzuki.”
かみのながいひと is our relative clause,
だいがくのとき is a plain modifer,
and ともだちのすずきさん identifies a person.
